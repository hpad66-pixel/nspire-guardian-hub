We do not currently know, with engineering certainty, what the stormwater system on this property is. Not how many catch basins. Not where the pipes run, what size they are, what they are made of, or whether they are intact. There is a retention pond of unquantified capacity and a second retention area that people reference but nobody has located. Every one of those unknowns is a flood-risk unknown. So the Stormwater Management Plan is written first — not last. It establishes the design basis, the level of service the property will be held to, and the investigation program required to get there. Every project that follows is then a defined chapter of that plan rather than a standalone expense, and the plan is reissued as a final governing document once the data is in.

Why this matters

Flooding is the fastest way this property generates a life-safety event, a resident claim, an insurance loss, and a regulatory finding — all at once. Writing the plan first means every dollar spent afterwards has a stated purpose inside a document the owner, the engineer and the regulator all read the same way.

STM-01Planning · EngineeringFirst deliverable

Stormwater Management Plan

Write the plan first. It defines the design basis, the level of service, and every project that follows.

This is the program's first deliverable, and putting it first is a deliberate choice. A management plan written at the end is a filing exercise. Written at the beginning, it is the instrument that governs everything: it states what the stormwater system is meant to achieve, what standard the property will be held to, what has to be investigated to get there, and in what order. Every project after it becomes a defined chapter of a plan the owner, the engineer and the regulator all read the same way — rather than a series of separate expenses nobody can connect to each other. The plan is issued in two parts: the framework and roadmap now, and the final governing document once the investigation and modelling results are in.

Scope of Work
  • Issue A — Framework and roadmap. Establish the design basis, the regulatory framework, and the adopted level of service the property will be held to for each design storm.
  • Define the stormwater system as currently understood, and state explicitly and in writing what is not known.
  • Define the investigation program required to close each gap, and set the sequence and dependencies between the projects that follow.
  • Establish the asset identification convention so every structure and pipe carries a permanent identifier from the first day of field work.
  • Define the performance criteria against which the finished system will be judged.
  • Issue B — Final governing plan. Incorporate the investigation, survey, geotechnical, modelling and design results as they arrive.
  • Consolidate the complete record set: drawings, calculations, permits, test data and certifications, into one governing document.
  • Document the implementation sequence actually executed and the as-built configuration of the system.
  • Publish and maintain the plan in ProjOS as the live reference for the stormwater system.
Deliverables
  • Stormwater Management Plan — Issue A: framework, design basis, level of service, investigation roadmap
  • Project sequence and dependency map
  • Asset identification convention
  • Performance criteria
  • Stormwater Management Plan — Issue B: final governing document with the complete record set
  • Live plan published in ProjOS

Stormwater Records & File Review

Determine what documentation of the stormwater system actually exists before spending a dollar in the field.

This is the least expensive project in the stormwater bucket and it governs the cost of every project after it. If usable as-builts and drainage permits exist, large parts of the field program shrink. If they do not, we know immediately that field verification is the starting point rather than the fallback — and we scope and price accordingly.

Scope of Work
  • Search and retrieve records from the City of Opa-Locka, Miami-Dade DERM, Miami-Dade RER, the Water Control District, SFWMD and the building department.
  • Pursue original developer, architect and engineer files, plats, easements, drainage permits and prior modification permits.
  • Review historical aerials and Sanborn maps to identify the original drainage configuration and any later alterations.
  • Specifically pursue any record of the second retention area — its location, permitted volume and whether it was ever built.
  • Produce a records inventory and, more importantly, a gap register: what we have, what we do not have, and what each gap costs to close in the field.
Deliverables
  • Records inventory with source and reliability rating on every document
  • Gap register mapped to the field projects required to close each gap
  • Recommendation memo: documentation-led path or field-led path
  • Findings written into the Stormwater Management Plan

STM-03InvestigationNot started

CCTV Inspection of the Underground Stormwater System

Send a camera through every pipe, catch basin to catch basin, and find out what is actually down there.

Nothing downstream can be designed responsibly without this. It answers, with video evidence, the questions we currently cannot answer: how many catch basins do we have, where do the pipes run, what size are they, what are they made of, are they perforated or solid, and are any of them collapsed. It is a non-intrusive condition assessment — no digging, no excavation, no disruption to residents.

Scope of Work
  • Locate, open and inventory every drainage structure on the property; apply the permanent numbering convention set in the Stormwater Management Plan.
  • Clean and jet lines as required to obtain a usable inspection.
  • CCTV every reach, catch basin to catch basin, with continuous video and distance-referenced logging.
  • Record for every reach: pipe diameter, material, joint type, slope indication, and whether the pipe is perforated or solid.
  • Grade structural and operational condition to NASSCO PACP standard — cracking, deformation, offset joints, root intrusion, sediment depth, and full or partial collapse.
  • Flag illicit connections and any discharge that does not belong in a stormwater line.
  • Deliver a condition-ranked defect register that drives the repair and rehabilitation priority list.
  • Hand the pipe network geometry directly to the survey team so the survey is verifying a known network rather than discovering one.
Deliverables
  • Complete structure and pipe inventory with permanent IDs
  • Full CCTV video library, indexed and reach-referenced
  • NASSCO PACP condition report with defect coding
  • Condition-ranked defect and rehabilitation priority register
  • Network geometry file issued to survey

Topographic & Underground Utility Survey

Produce a signed and sealed survey of the site surface and the drainage network, informed by what the camera found.

The survey is sequenced deliberately after the CCTV rather than before it. Running it second means the surveyor is locating and elevating a network we already understand, rather than guessing at inverts and missing structures nobody knew existed. That sequencing is what makes the survey a foundation document instead of a partial one.

Scope of Work
  • Topographic survey of the full site: finished floor elevations of every building, roadway and parking crowns and gutters, swales, curbs and site boundaries.
  • Locate and elevate every drainage structure identified in the CCTV inventory — rim and invert on every pipe entering and leaving.
  • Tie the survey to NAVD 88 and to the Miami-Dade County control network.
  • Reconcile the surveyed network against the CCTV inventory and resolve every discrepancy before sealing.
  • Deliver signed and sealed drawings plus editable CAD, and a survey-grade base map that becomes the single geometric basis for all subsequent stormwater design.
Deliverables
  • Signed and sealed topographic and utility survey
  • Editable CAD (DWG/DXF) base map
  • Rim and invert schedule for every structure
  • Reconciliation memo against the CCTV inventory

STM-05InvestigationNot started

Geotechnical Investigation & Permeability Testing

Measure how fast this soil actually absorbs water, so the hydraulic model is built on site data rather than assumption.

South Florida drainage design lives or dies on infiltration rate and seasonal high water table. Both are site-specific and both are inputs the model cannot run without. Testing at a small number of representative locations, selected once we know where the system is, gives the model defensible numbers — and defensible numbers are what survive permit review.

Scope of Work
  • Select boring and test locations using the survey and CCTV results, targeting proposed exfiltration and retention areas.
  • Advance soil borings with continuous sampling and classification.
  • Perform in-situ permeability testing using the Miami-Dade accepted method to establish the hydraulic conductivity value.
  • Establish the seasonal high water table elevation at each location.
  • Deliver a geotechnical report with design-ready parameters, signed and sealed.
Deliverables
  • Signed and sealed geotechnical report
  • Hydraulic conductivity values by location
  • Seasonal high water table elevations
  • Design parameter summary issued to the modelling team

STM-06SurveyNot started

Retention Pond Survey

Establish the pond's real shape, depth and current storage volume — above and below the waterline.

The retention pond is the property's primary stormwater storage asset, and we do not know its current capacity. Sediment accumulates slowly and silently; the pond looks unchanged from the bank while the storage inside it disappears. A bathymetric and topographic survey establishes what capacity exists today against what capacity was permitted, and that difference is the dredging case.

Scope of Work
  • Topographic survey of the pond banks, top of bank, side slopes and control structure.
  • Bathymetric survey of the pond bottom on a defined grid, capturing the sediment surface.
  • Probe to establish the depth of accumulated sediment and the original design bottom where recoverable.
  • Survey and document the outfall control structure, including weir elevations and orifice dimensions.
  • Compute the current stage-storage relationship and compare it against the permitted design storage.
Deliverables
  • Pond topographic and bathymetric survey
  • Sediment thickness map
  • Current versus permitted stage-storage curve
  • Control structure documentation

STM-07AnalysisNot started

Digital Terrain Model & Dredge Volume Determination

Turn the pond survey into a three-dimensional model and compute exactly how much material has to come out.

Dredging is priced by the cubic yard. Without a computed volume, the owner is exposed to open-ended quantities and change orders. A digital terrain model built from the survey produces a defensible quantity before anyone mobilises, and it becomes the basis for the bid, for the disposal estimate, and for measuring the contractor's completion.

Scope of Work
  • Build a digital terrain model of the existing sediment surface and of the target design bottom.
  • Compute cut volumes between the two surfaces, with contingency banding.
  • Produce dredge limit drawings and target elevation contours for the contractor.
  • Establish the measurement and verification method for confirming completion and final quantities.
  • Model the post-dredge stage-storage curve to confirm the storage volume recovered.
Deliverables
  • Digital terrain model (existing and design surfaces)
  • Computed dredge volume with contingency band
  • Dredge limit and target elevation drawings
  • Measurement and verification protocol
  • Post-dredge stage-storage curve

STM-08Environmental · ConstructionNot started

Dredged Material Characterization, Profiling & Disposal

Sample and profile the sediment before it moves, so it goes to a facility that will legally accept it.

This has to happen before dredging, not after. Sediment from a pond adjacent to industrial neighbours cannot be assumed clean. Waste profiling determines the disposal pathway and the disposal cost, and those two facts can swing the dredging budget by a wide margin. Profiling first also protects the owner from the worst case in this kind of work: material excavated, staged on site, and then found to be unacceptable at the receiving facility.

Scope of Work
  • Develop a sampling and analysis plan appropriate to the pond and the surrounding land uses.
  • Collect representative sediment samples across the pond footprint and at depth.
  • Laboratory analysis for the contaminants of concern indicated by the adjacent land uses, including metals and petroleum constituents.
  • Prepare the waste profile and obtain written acceptance from the receiving facility before dredging begins.
  • Determine the disposal pathway and confirm hauling, manifesting and chain of custody requirements.
  • Manage transport and disposal during dredging, with full manifest documentation retained for the compliance record.
Deliverables
  • Sampling and analysis plan
  • Laboratory analytical results
  • Approved waste profile and written facility acceptance
  • Disposal pathway determination memo
  • Complete manifest and chain of custody file

Retention Pond Dredging

Remove the accumulated sediment and restore the pond to its design storage volume.

This is the physical recovery of storage capacity the property has lost to sedimentation. It is a construction project with a measured quantity, a defined target elevation and a verification survey at completion — not an open-ended clean-out.

Scope of Work
  • Procure and contract the dredging work against the computed volume and dredge limit drawings.
  • Mobilise, install turbidity and erosion controls, and establish the dewatering and staging area.
  • Dredge to the design bottom elevation within the defined limits.
  • Dewater the removed material to a transportable moisture content.
  • Restore banks and side slopes; stabilise and re-vegetate disturbed areas, including the littoral zone.
  • Post-dredge verification survey confirming target elevations and the storage volume recovered.
Deliverables

  • Post-dredge verification survey

STM-10AnalysisNot started

Comprehensive Hydrologic & Hydraulic Model

Model the whole property against the 5-year, 25-year and 100-year storms and find out where the water goes.

This is the analytical core of the stormwater bucket. Every field project before it exists to feed this model, and every design project after it is dictated by its results. It answers the question the owner actually cares about: in a real storm, does water enter the buildings, and are the streets passable. Modelling three return periods separates a nuisance event from a life-safety event and tells us which improvements are required rather than merely desirable.

Scope of Work
  • Build the model from the survey base map, the CCTV network geometry, the geotechnical permeability values and the pond stage-storage curve.
  • Calibrate against observed flooding history on the property where records or resident reports exist.
  • Run the 5-year, 25-year and 100-year design storms for the applicable durations.
  • Evaluate two protection criteria explicitly and separately: finished floor elevation protection for every building, and roadway passability for emergency access.
  • Identify every deficiency — undersized pipes, insufficient storage, inadequate pumping, low points and overland flow paths.
  • Test improvement scenarios and quantify the flood-risk reduction each one buys.
  • Determine whether the pond and pipe network alone can meet the required level of service, or whether supplemental features are needed.
  • Write the results into the Stormwater Management Plan as the governing design basis.
Deliverables
  • Calibrated hydrologic and hydraulic model
  • Flood maps and inundation depths for each return period
  • Finished floor and roadway risk assessment by building and by street
  • Deficiency register with hydraulic cause identified
  • Improvement scenario comparison with risk reduction quantified
  • Determination on the need for supplemental features

STM-11DesignNot started

Stormwater Lift Station — Adequacy Assessment, Siting & Design

Determine whether the pumping capacity serving the pond is adequate, whether it is in the right place, and design what it should be.

A retention pond only performs if what leaves it can leave fast enough. This project evaluates the existing pumping arrangement against modelled demand, tests whether its location relative to the pond is hydraulically correct, and produces a design for whatever the answer requires — rerating, relocating, or replacing.

Scope of Work
  • Assess the existing lift station: condition, capacity, controls, power supply and reliability.
  • Evaluate its location relative to the retention pond and to the modelled inflow points.
  • Determine required firm capacity against the modelled design storms.
  • Evaluate siting alternatives where relocation is indicated.
  • Produce design drawings and specifications for the selected configuration, including wet well, pumps, controls, standby power and telemetry.
  • Establish the permitting pathway and prepare permit-ready documents.
Deliverables
  • Adequacy and condition assessment report
  • Siting alternatives evaluation with recommendation
  • Signed and sealed design drawings and specifications
  • Permit-ready submittal package

Retention Pond Design & Reconfiguration

Design the pond to the storage volume the model requires, not the volume it happens to have.

Once the model states the storage volume this property needs in order to be protected in a design storm, the pond has to be designed to deliver it. That may mean deepening, expanding the footprint, reshaping side slopes for safety and maintenance access, or rebuilding the control structure.

Scope of Work
  • Design the pond to the target storage volume and stage-storage relationship established by the hydraulic model.
  • Design side slopes, safety shelf, littoral zone and maintenance access.
  • Design the outfall control structure — weir, orifice and discharge rate — to the permitted allowable discharge.
  • Integrate with the lift station design and confirm combined performance.
  • Prepare permit-ready drawings, calculations and specifications.
Deliverables
  • Signed and sealed pond design drawings
  • Stage-storage-discharge calculations
  • Control structure design
  • Permit submittal package

STM-13Design · Permit · ConstructNot started

Site Regrading & Recontouring — Design, Permit & Construct

Reshape the ground so that water goes where the model says it should go.

The model will identify low points, reverse slopes and blocked overland flow paths that no pipe can fix. Regrading is how a property is made to drain by gravity before any mechanical system is asked to help. This is the largest construction item in the program and is scoped, phased and priced directly from the model results.

Scope of Work
  • Design regrading and recontouring to achieve the drainage patterns and hydraulic capacity established by the model.
  • Set finished floor protection and roadway crown elevations to the adopted level of service.
  • Design conveyance improvements — swales, inlets, pipe upsizing — where the model identifies capacity deficiency.
  • Permit through Miami-Dade DERM, SFWMD and the City of Opa-Locka.
  • Phase construction to maintain resident access, parking and emergency vehicle circulation throughout.
  • Construct, with survey verification of finished grades against design.
  • Restore paving, landscaping and site features.
Deliverables
  • Signed and sealed grading and drainage drawings
  • Approved permits
  • Phased construction plan with resident access maintained
  • Constructed improvements with as-built survey verification
  • Record drawings issued into the Stormwater Management Plan

STM-14Investigation · Design · ConstructConditional

Supplemental Stormwater Features — Exfiltration Trench & the Second Retention Area

If the model says the pond and pipes are not enough, add capacity — and finally settle the question of the second retention area.

This project is deliberately conditional. It is triggered only if the hydraulic model demonstrates that the primary system cannot meet the required level of service. Two options are on the table. The first is an exfiltration trench system, which is the standard Miami-Dade answer and depends directly on the permeability values from the geotechnical work. The second is the second retention area — a feature everybody on this property references and nobody has located. That ambiguity is itself a liability, and this project closes it: we either find it, confirm it, and restore it to function, or we establish on the record that it does not exist.

Scope of Work
  • Locate and confirm the second retention area through records, historical aerials, geophysical survey and field investigation.
  • If found: assess its condition, permitted status and residual storage capacity, and determine whether restoration is viable and cost-effective.
  • If not found: document the investigation and formally close the question so it stops appearing as an open assumption in every future analysis.
  • Design an exfiltration trench system sized to the residual storage deficit, using the measured hydraulic conductivity from the geotechnical work.
  • Compare the two options on capacity delivered, cost, permitability and long-term maintenance burden.
  • Permit and construct the selected option.
Deliverables
  • Second retention area investigation report — located and assessed, or formally closed out
  • Exfiltration trench design and calculations
  • Options comparison with recommendation
  • Permits and constructed improvements
Budget

STM-15Operations · DocumentationNot started

Stormwater Operations Plan

Turn the plan into a running operation — the same way the sewer extension was operationalized.

A management plan describes the system. An operations plan runs it. This is the document that survives staff turnover: who inspects what, how often, what triggers a response, what gets recorded, and what gets reported to whom. It follows exactly the model already proven on the sewer side of this property.

Scope of Work
  • Define routine inspection and maintenance procedures for every asset class — catch basins, pipes, pond, control structure, lift station and any supplemental features.
  • Set inspection frequencies, cleaning cycles and sediment removal triggers, so storage loss is caught early rather than rediscovered in a decade.
  • Establish pre-storm readiness and post-storm response procedures.
  • Define the regulatory reporting calendar and the responsible party for each submittal.
  • Establish record-keeping, evidence retention and the annual system condition review.
  • Define performance monitoring, alarm thresholds and escalation paths.
  • Build the operating record into ProjOS with scheduled tasks, evidence capture and automatic compliance reporting.
Deliverables
  • Stormwater Operations Plan
  • Inspection and maintenance schedule by asset class
  • Pre-storm and post-storm response procedures
  • Regulatory reporting calendar with named responsibility
  • ProjOS operating record with scheduled tasks and evidence capture
  • Staff training and handover
